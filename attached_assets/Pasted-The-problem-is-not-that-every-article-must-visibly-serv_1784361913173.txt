The problem is not that every article must visibly serve everyone. The real editorial gap is that the system currently has no requirement to consider the wider stakeholder impact before deciding what belongs in the article.

My editorial recommendation

Insights should follow:

One primary reader. One primary problem. Multiple lenses only when they materially improve understanding.

The signature of Insights should not be:

Every article contains three equally sized sections for employers, employees and staffing firms.

It should be:

Insights does not examine important workforce changes from only one side of the table.

That is a significant difference.

What current market research indicates

LinkedIn’s thought-leadership guidance says content should be accurate, digestible, actionable and, most importantly here, relevant to a defined audience. It explicitly recommends “zeroing in on the right audience” when a business serves several personas rather than trying to make every asset equally relevant to everyone.

The Edelman–LinkedIn research also found that high-quality thought leadership succeeds when it:

Uses strong research and data.
Helps the reader understand their challenges and opportunities.
Provides concrete guidance and examples.

Notice that the standard is not “cover every possible stakeholder.” It is to help the intended reader understand a problem clearly and act on it.

The same study found that recognizable expertise and a distinctive format contribute to perceived quality, reinforcing your real-author and editorial-structure strategy.

Therefore, the research supports audience focus plus differentiated perspective, not mandatory equal-length multi-audience coverage.

The correct Insights model
1. Every article must have a primary lens

Before writing, the editor should declare:

Primary audience.
Primary question.
Desired reader outcome.
Why this audience needs the article now.

For example:

Primary audience: Healthcare employers
Question: Should we use AI screening for clinical roles?
Outcome: Help the employer decide where AI can assist and where human review remains necessary.

Approximately 70–80% of the article should remain focused on that reader.

2. Every topic should receive an internal stakeholder scan

The author or editorial system should still ask:

How does this affect employers?
How does this affect employees or candidates?
How does this affect recruiters, staffing organizations or MSP/VMS partners?

This scan should be mandatory during planning.

But the result does not always have to appear in full in the published article.

The scan prevents one-sided conclusions. The editor then decides whether the secondary perspectives are:

Essential to include.
Worth mentioning briefly.
Better handled in a companion article.
Not materially relevant.

That is editorial intelligence, rather than checklist writing.

3. Use three publishing modes
Mode A: Single-lens article

Best for practical guides, narrow questions and role-specific advice.

Examples:

“How candidates should prepare for AI-assisted interviews.”
“Five questions employers should ask before adopting an AI screening tool.”
“What healthcare recruiters should verify before submitting a travel nurse.”

These should remain tightly focused on one reader.

A short closing note can acknowledge another affected party, but it should not interrupt the main narrative.

Mode B: Primary lens plus counterparty lens

Best when one stakeholder’s decision directly affects another.

Example:

Topic: Employers using automated candidate rejection.

The article might be 75% employer-focused, followed by a small section:

What candidates will experience

This makes the employer guidance more responsible without changing the intended reader.

Mode C: Full system or three-lens Insight

Best for major structural changes affecting the entire employment ecosystem.

Examples:

AI’s effect on hiring trust.
Skills-based hiring replacing degree requirements.
Changes in healthcare credentialing.
Pay-transparency regulations.
The effect of automation on recruiter roles.
Major workforce shortages or labor-market shifts.

These are the articles where the three-lens structure becomes an Insights signature because the subject cannot be understood responsibly through one perspective.

Recommended editorial rule

Replace:

Every article must address employers, employees/candidates and staffing partners.

With:

Every topic must be evaluated across the employer, employee/candidate and staffing-partner lenses. The published article must identify one primary audience and include additional lenses only when they materially improve the reader’s understanding, reveal consequences or prevent a one-sided conclusion.

Then add:

For system-level or industry-shift articles, all materially affected stakeholder lenses must be represented. For tactical or audience-specific articles, the primary lens should remain dominant and secondary perspectives may be summarized, linked as companion content or omitted with editorial justification.

A practical size rule

To keep content focused:

Article type	Primary lens	Secondary lenses
Audience-specific guide	85–100%	Usually omitted or one brief note
Decision article	70–80%	20–30% combined
System-shift article	50–65%	Remaining space divided by relevance
Short Field Signal	One lens	One sentence only when material
Flagship research article	Dominant lens plus complete impact analysis	All affected groups represented

Do not automatically assign one-third of the article to each stakeholder. Equal space is rarely equal relevance.

A better signature format

For major articles, use a compact Stakeholder Impact section rather than three additional essays:

What this means across the hiring system

For employers:
What changes in decisions, risk, cost or process.

For employees and candidates:
What changes in opportunity, expectations, transparency or preparation.

For staffing partners:
What changes in delivery, communication, compliance or service expectations.

Each lens may require only two or three meaningful sentences.

This preserves the distinctiveness of Insights without bloating the article.

Example

Consider an article titled:

AI screening may improve speed—but who is accountable when it rejects the wrong candidate?

The primary audience could be employers.

The body would focus on:

Governance.
Quality controls.
Validation.
Human review.
Documentation.
Decision accountability.

Near the end:

Candidate impact: Candidates need clearer notice, understandable criteria and a route to question material errors.

Staffing impact: Agencies will need to understand how their candidates are screened and avoid treating an unexplained algorithmic rejection as a complete assessment of suitability.

The additional lenses strengthen the employer article because they reveal consequences the employer must understand. They do not compete with the main audience.

Final editorial judgment

Do not require three fully developed lenses in every article. That would become formulaic and could damage engagement.

Require instead:

One clearly defined primary audience.
A mandatory three-lens editorial assessment.
Publication of secondary lenses only when materially relevant.
Full multi-lens treatment for structural and ecosystem-level subjects.
Companion articles when each audience deserves substantial depth.

The true Insights differentiator is not “we put three sections in every article.”

It is:

We begin with the reader’s real problem, but we understand that hiring decisions affect people on every side—and we do not hide those consequences.

That is focused enough to retain reader interest and broad-minded enough to build the trusted Insights brand you are envisioning.
