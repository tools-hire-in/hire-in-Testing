# Enterprise GSA Per Diem Calculator Design for Travel Staffing

## What the law supports

The core legal point is this: a **GSA rate is a reimbursement ceiling used for deemed substantiation**, not an automatic entitlement for a private employer to pay that exact amount to every traveler. For tax-free treatment, the arrangement still has to satisfy the IRS accountable plan rules: the expense must have a business connection, the employee must substantiate time, place, and business purpose within a reasonable period, and excess amounts must be returned or treated as wages. Under the IRS safe harbors, substantiation within **60 days** and return of excess within **120 days** are treated as timely. If the plan fails those rules, the amounts are treated as wages and become subject to withholding and employment taxes. citeturn8search3turn8search7turn11view0turn6search7

For travel stipends, the tax anchor is not “how many shifts were worked,” but whether the employee is **traveling away from a tax home** on a **temporary assignment** and incurring business travel expenses. IRS Publication 463 says a taxpayer is away from home if the duties require being away from the tax home substantially longer than an ordinary day’s work and the traveler must sleep or rest to meet work demands. It also says that if an assignment away from the main place of work is temporary, the worker is considered away from home for the whole period away from the main place of work; generally, a single-location assignment expected to last **one year or less** is temporary, while an assignment expected to last **more than one year** is indefinite and becomes the new tax home. citeturn10view0turn10view1

That is why the common staffing question — **“If the nurse works 36 hours, can we pay per diem for 7 days or only 3 days?”** — does **not** have a universal black-letter answer of “always 7” or “always 3.” The more defensible reading of the IRS travel rules is this: if the traveler remains away from the tax home for the week and continues to incur duplicate lodging and meal/incidental expenses at the assignment location, a full-week reimbursement can be supportable; if the traveler goes back to the tax home on days off, the IRS says the traveler is not away from home while in the hometown, although certain travel and retained lodging costs may still be deductible up to limits. In other words, the legally safer rule is to pay by **substantiated travel status by calendar day**, not by scheduled shifts alone. That conclusion is an inference from the IRS travel-day rules, not a separate statute written specifically for travel nurses. citeturn10view0turn10view1

There is also an important wage-and-hour overlay. Under federal overtime law, most nonexempt employees must receive overtime after **40 hours in a workweek**, and the workweek is a fixed period of **168 hours** that stands alone; there is no averaging of multiple workweeks. The FLSA is only the federal floor, and the Department of Labor notes that employees may have greater protections under state or local law. For nursing care facilities, federal law also allows an optional **“8 and 80”** system if the prerequisites are met. Your calculator therefore needs a federal baseline plus state-and-facility overlays rather than one hard-coded OT rule. citeturn5search0turn5search2turn5search8turn14search14

A second wage-and-hour risk is disguised wages. Federal regular-rate rules exclude reimbursements only when they are a **reasonable approximation** of expenses incurred for the employer’s benefit. The Department of Labor has said employees must actually incur the expenses for exclusion to apply, and the IRS has long taken the view that reimbursements tied to hours worked or structured as a relabeling of compensation do not satisfy accountable plan requirements. That is why your product should never let recruiters “solve margin” by moving money between taxable hourly pay and non-taxable stipend in a way that tracks hours worked or productivity. citeturn3search1turn3search2turn3search8turn9search0turn9search1turn9search3

## The correct decision logic for 36-hour assignments

For a travel nurse working **3 x 12-hour shifts**, the calculator should ask a compliance question before it computes any stipend: **Was the traveler away from the tax home and incurring substantiated travel expenses for all 7 calendar days, or only for certain days?** That is the decisive branch. The tool should explicitly separate **schedule hours** from **travel-away days** so recruiters do not confuse payroll rules with travel substantiation rules. This separation is consistent with the IRS’s travel-away framework and with the DOL’s rule that overtime is based on hours worked in a workweek, not on how a company labels reimbursements. citeturn10view0turn5search2turn3search1

A practical enterprise rule set for the tool would look like this:

| Scenario | Recommended stipend logic | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Traveler stays at assignment housing all week and remains away from tax home | Allow **7 calendar days** of lodging and M&IE, subject to accountable plan documentation and company policy | IRS treats temporary assignment travel as away from home for the period away from the main place of work; weekly lodging/meal duplication can continue between shifts citeturn10view0 |
| Traveler works 3 shifts, then returns to tax home for days off | Default to **3 travel days** for M&IE and lodging only for substantiated away days, unless retained lodging rules apply | IRS says traveler is not away from home while in hometown on days off; retained lodging may still need separate treatment citeturn10view0 |
| Traveler keeps temporary lodging at assignment site while briefly returning home | Apply **retained lodging** logic and cap home-return reimbursement appropriately | IRS allows hotel-room retention in certain temporary-assignment day-off situations, with limits tied to what staying would have cost citeturn10view0 |
| Stipend amount changes when hours worked change | **Do not allow** as a tax-free stipend rule | IRS and DOL both flag hour-based “reimbursements” as wage-like and risky citeturn9search0turn9search3turn3search2 |

This is the single most important product decision for your recruiters: the tool should compute **stipend eligibility by travel day status**, not by “36 hours means 3 days” or “36 hours means a full week.” A well-designed system should force the recruiter or payroll user to select one of three travel patterns for each week: **full-week away**, **partial-week away**, or **returned home**, and then preserve that choice in the audit trail. That gives you a defensible compliance record and avoids casual overpayment. citeturn10view0turn8search0turn8search7

Using the California example visible in your current worksheet, the GSA FY 2026 rate for **Santa Rosa, Sonoma County** is **$157 lodging + $86 M&IE = $243 per day**, and GSA’s first/last-day rule yields a reduced M&IE amount of **$64.50** for a partial travel day. So a traveler away all week would have a raw weekly cap of **$1,701** before any company-specific reductions, while a three-day-away pattern would have a raw cap of **$729**. This is exactly why the system must have a travel-days field rather than assuming that a 36-hour schedule automatically maps to either outcome. citeturn15search0turn2search1turn11view0

## The calculator specification you should build

Based on the spreadsheet image you shared, the strongest product direction is to keep your existing commercial structure — **Basic Information, Billing, Candidate Pay, Expenses, Profit** — but put a formal rules engine underneath it so the spreadsheet logic becomes a controlled enterprise application rather than a fragile workbook.

At the data-input level, the calculator should require these fields for every quote: **assignment ZIP code**, **work location state/county**, **travel start date**, **travel end date**, **week-begin date**, **scheduled hours**, **actual hours**, **travel-away pattern**, **tax home attestation status**, **housing method** (stipend, direct bill, employer-provided, none), **employee class** (travel nurse, allied health, IT contractor, engineer, exempt computer employee, engineering technician, etc.), and **jurisdiction overlay**. GSA itself says reimbursement is based on the **location of the work activities and not the accommodations**, unless lodging is not available at the work activity and a different rate is authorized, and many GSA localities have **seasonal monthly lodging rates**, so ZIP and date are not optional fields in a compliant tool. citeturn15search0turn7view0

The core calculation layer should be a rules engine with these steps:

| Step | Logic |
|---|---|
| Rate lookup | Pull GSA rate by ZIP and fiscal year; resolve county/locality and month-specific lodging |
| Travel-day determination | Compute full days and partial days by calendar date, then apply company policy based on substantiated travel pattern |
| Per diem cap calculation | `weekly_lodging_cap + weekly_M&IE_cap`, with first/last travel-day proration for M&IE when applicable |
| Wage classification | Determine whether the worker is nonexempt or exempt under the role/jurisdiction pack |
| Payroll calculation | Compute regular earnings, overtime, shift differential, call-back, holiday, orientation, bonuses |
| Regular-rate check | Exclude bona fide reimbursements; include anything that functions as wage compensation |
| Margin calculation | Compute bill revenue, payroll burden, stipend, fees, and net margin |
| Compliance output | Show source rates, rule version, assumptions, warnings, and approver requirement |

The formula design should deliberately **decouple stipend and hourly wage**. In the UI, the user can still see a “weekly non-taxable” number and an “hourly blended” number, because that helps recruiters sell offers, but those view calculations must be downstream of the compliance engine, not editable drivers. Otherwise, users will try to back into a target blended rate by manipulating the non-taxable component, which is exactly the behavior most likely to create accountable-plan and regular-rate problems. citeturn9search0turn9search3turn3search1turn3search8

Your role packs should reflect sector differences from day one. For **healthcare**, many travel nurses are paid hourly and are therefore nonexempt for federal overtime purposes, even though registered nurses can qualify for the learned professional exemption if they meet the duties test and are paid on a salary basis at the required level; nursing care facilities may also use the 8-and-80 option. For **IT**, the computer employee exemption can apply only if the duties test is met and the employee is paid on a salary basis or at least **$27.63 per hour**. For **engineering**, learned-professional analysis may apply to true engineers, but DOL guidance specifically notes that **engineering technicians** generally are not exempt. Those distinctions should be delivered as selectable rule packs, not as recruiter memory. citeturn14search0turn14search14turn13search0turn13search1turn18search1turn18search6

The output screen should show three numbers separately: **legal max stipend**, **company-approved stipend**, and **proposed recruiter package**. If the recruiter proposal exceeds the legal max or conflicts with the travel-day logic, the tool should block submission or reroute for payroll/compliance approval. That design is especially important because the IRS treats excess per diem over the deemed substantiated amount as wages, and the DOL looks through labels to determine whether a payment is really compensation. citeturn11view0turn8search7turn9search1

## The data and update architecture that will keep it current

For rates, the cleanest primary source is the **GSA Per Diem API**. GSA documents six primary endpoints, including search by **city**, **state**, **ZIP**, full **CONUS lodging**, full **CONUS M&IE**, and a **ZIP-to-destination mapping** file. GSA says the API is generally updated with new fiscal-year rates in **mid-August**, rates are grouped by **fiscal year**, and the default rate limit is **1,000 requests per hour**. GSA also publishes downloadable FY rate files and a ZIP code developer file, which should be your fallback ingestion source if the API is unavailable. citeturn7view0turn1search3turn1search8

Your architecture should therefore have a **read-only rates service** with versioned snapshots. The safest production model is:

| Layer | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Source ingestion | Nightly pull from GSA API; weekly checksum against GSA downloadable files |
| Versioning | Immutable rate tables by fiscal year, locality, month, and source timestamp |
| Quote engine | Uses the latest approved snapshot, never live web results directly |
| Exception handling | If API fails, fall back to last approved snapshot and raise an alert |
| Change control | Major annual rollover in August/September requires compliance re-certification |
| Auditability | Every quote stores rate source, snapshot version, locality resolution, and assumptions |

That matters because GSA itself warns that per diem localities can change and tells users to check the site for the most current rate. A top-tier staffing company should therefore never let a recruiter quote directly off a live lookup result without preserving the exact version of the rate table used at quote time. citeturn6search2turn7view0

Legal-rule updates are different from rate updates. There is no single official API that will keep your entire travel-staffing compliance model current across IRS, DOL, and fifty-state overlays. That means you should build a **curated rules registry** rather than pretending there is one perfect endpoint. In practice, that registry should ingest IRS travel-accountable-plan sources, DOL wage-and-hour guidance, and your own approved internal interpretations, and every rule should have an effective date, jurisdiction, citation set, owner, and approval status. citeturn10view0turn8search7turn5search2

The AI capability should sit **behind** that registry, not replace it. The strongest design is a **retrieval-first assistant** that can answer questions like “Why is this week capped at 3 travel days?” or “Why did the system block 7 lodging days for this 36-hour assignment?” by citing the exact internal rule version and the linked official sources. NIST’s AI RMF and the GenAI profile both emphasize governance, legal/regulatory alignment, risk-based controls, and ongoing evaluation for generative AI systems. For a pay-sensitive calculator, AI should be allowed to **explain, summarize, and draft**, but not to silently change compensation logic in production. citeturn16view0turn4search0turn4search12

## Security, compliance, and AI governance

Because this product touches compensation, margin, recruiter behavior, and potentially employee travel attestations, it should be designed like a controlled payroll-adjacent system, not a spreadsheet migration. NIST SP 800-53 is a strong control baseline for this kind of application because it provides a catalog of security and privacy controls for protecting systems, organizations, and individuals from a broad range of threats and privacy risks. In practical product terms, that means role-based access, segregation of duties, immutable audit logging, encryption in transit and at rest, secure key management, environment separation, and formal change management for rules and rate tables. citeturn17search0turn17search1turn17search7

The minimum enterprise operating model should include four control gates. First, **recruiters** can create packages but cannot override legal caps. Second, **payroll/compliance** can approve or deny exceptions. Third, **system administrators** can manage users but cannot rewrite compensation rules without a separate approval path. Fourth, every change to a rule, threshold, or stipend policy must be versioned with a reason code and an approver. Those are design recommendations, but they align directly with the kind of enterprise control environment reflected in NIST and SOC frameworks. citeturn17search0turn12search0turn12search3

For healthcare use, HIPAA should be treated as a **conditional overlay**, not automatically assumed. HHS explains that the HIPAA Privacy Rule protects **individually identifiable health information** held or transmitted by covered entities and business associates, and the Security Rule applies to **electronic protected health information**. If your tool only contains recruiter, assignment, payroll, and rate data, HIPAA may not be the primary legal regime for the calculator itself; if users begin attaching medical restrictions, diagnosis-linked accommodation notes, or other PHI/ePHI, then HIPAA controls become much more relevant. The product should therefore minimize data collection and explicitly prohibit storing PHI unless that use case is separately designed and governed. citeturn4search2turn4search11

The AI assistant should also have hard boundaries. It should never be permitted to directly edit rate tables, approve overrides, or classify a payment as tax-free without a rules-engine basis. The safer pattern is: **rules engine decides, AI explains**. NIST’s GenAI guidance explicitly recommends aligning development and use with applicable laws and regulations, integrating trustworthy-AI characteristics into policies and procedures, and maintaining updated risk management processes over the AI lifecycle. For your setting, that means prompt logging, output monitoring, approval workflows for policy changes, and periodic red-teaming around wage, privacy, and data-leakage failure modes. citeturn16view0

## The professional product blueprint I would recommend

The best final product for a top-tier staffing, staff augmentation, or RPO firm is a **quote-to-approval compensation workbench** with five primary panels that mirror the strengths of your current sheet but make the workflow enterprise-safe.

The first panel should be **Assignment Setup**. This is where the recruiter enters facility/client, role family, ZIP, dates, schedule, worker class, and travel pattern. The system then immediately resolves the GSA locality, month-specific lodging, M&IE, and any sector-jurisdiction pack. GSA’s own rate structure makes this step essential because lodging rates may differ by month and locality, and reimbursement is driven by work location. citeturn15search0turn7view0

The second panel should be **Stipend Eligibility and Per Diem**. This panel should show the raw GSA daily caps, the weekly cap based on selected travel-away days, first/last-day proration, and any company reduction policy. It should also surface a plain-English note such as: *“7-day stipend selected because traveler is attested as away from tax home for the full week and retains assignment lodging.”* or *“3-day stipend selected because traveler returns home on off days.”* That explanation should be generated from the rules engine and saved with the quote for audit purposes. citeturn10view0turn11view0turn8search0

The third panel should be **Taxable Pay and Overtime**. Here the tool calculates base hourly pay, shift differentials, overtime, call-back, holiday, and orientation. For healthcare, it should support both a standard 40-hour workweek and a nursing-facility 8-and-80 mode when legally appropriate. For IT and engineering, it should offer an exemption-status workflow instead of assuming every worker is hourly. The DOL’s role-specific guidance makes this kind of structured branching much safer than generic staffing logic. citeturn5search0turn14search14turn13search0turn18search1

The fourth panel should be **Business Economics**. This is where your current spreadsheet is already strong. Keep bill rate, VMS fee, payroll taxes, bonuses, orientation cost, and contract margin, but add two safeguards: a **margin sensitivity slider** that cannot alter the legal stipend cap, and a **compliance risk indicator** that turns from green to amber to red if the recruiter is getting too close to a wage-recharacterization pattern. That risk score should be triggered by factors such as unusually low taxable base wage, stipend-to-taxable ratio spikes, mismatched travel-day selections, or repeated manual overrides. That risk logic is a product recommendation, but it is aimed at the exact legal pattern that IRS and DOL sources warn about. citeturn9search0turn9search1turn3search2

The fifth panel should be **Audit, Citations, and Approval**. A top-tier company should be able to open any quote six months later and see: the exact GSA source version used, the locality resolution, the user who made the quote, the rule version for stipend-day logic, the worker classification rules applied, the reason for any override, and the approver who accepted the package. That is the layer that turns a recruiter tool into an enterprise control system. It is also where the AI assistant becomes genuinely valuable, because it can answer user questions with source-backed explanations instead of informal Slack guidance. citeturn7view0turn8search7turn16view0

If you build it this way, the product will work not only for travel nursing but also for allied healthcare, IT staffing, and engineering staffing. The universal core is the same: **jurisdiction-aware payroll logic, substantiated travel-day logic, versioned GSA rate data, and controlled recruiter packaging**. What changes by sector is the exemption pack, the overtime overlay, and the standard policy templates.
