This plan is genuinely solid — the review feedback already caught the biggest landmine (synchronous send after approval), and the corrected async design with per-recipient delivery records is exactly how mature systems do it. So instead of re-praising what's right, let me give you the review a staff-level notifications engineer would give: the remaining gaps, roughly in order of how much they'll hurt you.
1. The setImmediate worker has no crash recovery — this is your biggest remaining risk. The plan explicitly avoids external queue infrastructure, which is the right call at your scale. But fire-and-forget on the same process means: admin approves, worker starts delivering, your server restarts (deploy, crash, OOM), and the blast is now stuck in delivering forever. Nobody gets emails, and the UI shows "Delivering…" for eternity. The fix is cheap: on server startup (and/or on a 5-minute interval), sweep for blasts stuck in delivering or approved older than some timeout and re-enqueue processBlast for them. Since the worker already only processes pending delivery records, resuming is safe by design. This one function turns a fragile design into a robust one without adding Redis or SQS.
2. There's a small duplicate-send window even per-recipient. If the process dies between SendGrid accepting an email and your DB write marking that row sent, a retry resends that one email. At your volume, I'd consciously accept this — one duplicate HR reminder is harmless — but document the decision. If you ever care, pass the delivery record ID as a SendGrid custom_arg and reconcile via their event webhook. Don't build that now.
3. Pending blasts go stale. A governance-overdue blast queued Friday at 6am might sit until Monday. By then some recipients have completed their items, someone has left the company, and the content is wrong — which recreates the exact "immature notifications" problem that started this. Two additions:

Re-validate recipients at delivery time: skip rows where the user is deactivated or (ideally) where the triggering condition no longer holds.
Auto-expire: if a blast isn't reviewed within 48–72 hours, flip it to cancelled with reason expired and let the next cron run re-queue fresh content. This is better than sending stale data late.

4. No escalation path if admins don't notice the badge. A passive sidebar badge is easy to miss. Since some of these crons are compliance-related (probation, governance), a silently stuck queue is itself a risk. Send a single-recipient transactional email (which bypasses the queue) to super admins when a blast has been pending more than, say, 4 hours.
5. Edit & Approve needs an audit trail. If an admin edits the subject/body before approving, keep original_subject/original_body columns (or an edits JSONB). In a staffing company, where these emails may touch contractors and compliance deadlines, "who changed what was sent" is a question you will eventually be asked.
6. Verify the crons actually batch. queueBlast triggers on recipients.length >= threshold — but if a cron loops over users and sends one email per iteration, every call looks like a single-recipient email and slips past the queue. Step 4 needs to confirm each cron aggregates its full recipient list into one queueBlast call per run.
For your staffing company specifically, two things matter more than they would at Meta scale:
The review queue stops accidents; it doesn't fix noisiness. Your original complaint was that the system is "too aggressive" — and the queue only catches bulk blasts. The steady-state annoyance of one employee getting five separate reminder emails in a week is solved by digesting (bundle per-employee notifications into one daily/weekly summary), which the plan correctly scoped out but which I'd make the very next task. Queue first (it's your safety net), digest second (it's your actual UX fix).
Don't over-build. You do not need Kafka, SQS, BullMQ, or webhooks-based delivery tracking at this scale. The in-process worker + recovery sweep handles you comfortably up to thousands of recipients per blast. Sequential sending is fine; if a blast ever exceeds ~500 recipients, add a small concurrency limit (5 at a time) with backoff on SendGrid 429s — a ten-line change later.
So my verdict: approve the plan as written, but add three things before calling it done — the startup recovery sweep for stuck delivering blasts, blast auto-expiry with recipient re-validation, and the edit audit columns. Those are maybe a day of extra work combined and they close every failure mode that would embarrass you in front of your team a second time.
