## BD Agent + Deck Builder — Field Operations Manual

**Audience:** Business Development team members

**Purpose:** Get you from "I have a meeting Thursday" to "I'm the most prepared person in that room" in under 20 minutes.

## Before You Start: Why Not Just Use ChatGPT?

This is the right question. Here is the honest answer.

ChatGPT is a general-purpose language model. It is very good at writing. It knows nothing about your deals.

The BD Agent is different in five specific ways:

**1. It knows the Buyer Stage Model.**
The agent classifies every prospect into one of five buying stages and adjusts every recommendation — questions, proof points, deck sections, next steps — to match where the buyer actually is. ChatGPT will give you a polished list of discovery questions regardless of whether the buyer has never heard of staffing agencies or is three weeks from signing a master agreement.

**2. It knows the Buyer Decision Model.**
The agent organizes its advice around what the buyer must decide at each stage: Is the problem real? Which vendor fits? Have the commercial terms been validated? ChatGPT will answer the question you type. The BD Agent will answer the question the deal actually needs.

**3. It knows Hire'in's approved capabilities.**
Every output from the BD Agent is grounded in what Hire'in can truthfully claim — healthcare staffing disciplines, IT delivery models, MSP/VMS operating experience, credentialing awareness, submission quality controls. ChatGPT will generate confident-sounding claims about staffing that may have no connection to what your company actually does.

**4. It knows your published Insights articles.**
When a prospect raises an objection or pain point, the BD Agent retrieves relevant published articles from the Hire'in Insights library and suggests them as proof points. ChatGPT has no access to your content.

**5. It knows your Master Decks.**
The agent can take any Master Deck and customize every slide for a specific client, buyer stage, and positioning angle — in minutes. ChatGPT cannot open your deck, let alone rewrite it coherently.

**Concrete example.** You are preparing for a first call with a hospital group's VP of Nursing. You type the same prompt into both:

> "Help me prepare for a discovery meeting with a hospital VP of Nursing who is struggling with travel nurse cost and turnover."

**ChatGPT output:** A list of eight generic discovery questions about budget, current vendors, satisfaction, timeline. Well-written. Could apply to any staffing call in any industry.

**BD Agent output (Account Discovery + Meeting Preparation mode):** A buyer-stage classification (Problem Identification — they know costs are high but haven't yet evaluated vendors), a stakeholder analysis noting that a VP of Nursing is an operational sponsor but likely not the economic buyer, five targeted discovery questions designed to surface the gap between current submission quality and actual fill outcomes, two published Insights articles on healthcare workforce continuity and credentialing discipline as conversation starters, and a recommended next step: schedule a joint call with the CFO or COO before proposing a model.

That is the difference. Now let's cover every feature.

## Part 1: The Buyer Stage Model

### The Five Stages

**Stage 1 — Problem Identification**
The buyer knows something is wrong but has not yet begun evaluating solutions or vendors. They are experiencing symptoms: high agency spend, slow fills, compliance issues, turnover. They are not yet shopping for a staffing partner and may not know they need one.

*What they say:* "Our fill times are brutal right now." / "We've been burning through travel nurses." / "HR is completely overwhelmed."

*What the agent does:* Focuses on pain framing. Asks questions to make the cost of inaction concrete. Does not pitch a solution — the buyer is not ready to evaluate one.

**Stage 2 — Solution Exploration**
The buyer has defined the problem and is exploring what a solution looks like. They are asking "what are my options" rather than "which vendor." They may be researching staffing models, MSP programs, direct-hire versus contract.

*What they say:* "We're trying to figure out whether we should go MSP or direct vendor." / "Tell me how other health systems handle this." / "What does an embedded team model look like?"

*What the agent does:* Leads with operating model education. Positions Hire'in as a delivery partner with a clear methodology, not a transactional vendor. Emphasizes the Hire'in AI-and-human operating model.

**Stage 3 — Vendor Evaluation**
The buyer is actively comparing specific vendors. They have defined their requirements and are now assessing which partner best meets them. Reference checks, proposal comparisons, and panel presentations happen here.

*What they say:* "We're talking to three agencies." / "Can you give us references from comparable engagements?" / "Walk us through your credentialing process." / "What's your fill rate for travel nursing?"

*What the agent does:* Emphasizes proof, differentiation, and risk reduction. Prepares you for the five most common objections. Recommends content that reduces implementation risk in the buyer's mind — quality controls, audit trail, escalation process — not just capability claims.

**Stage 4 — Commercial Validation**
The buyer has effectively selected a vendor (likely you) and is now pressure-testing the commercial terms. Rate negotiations, SLA definitions, contractual scope, legal review.

*What they say:* "Your rate is 12% above what our incumbent charges." / "We need a 48-hour fill guarantee in the contract." / "Can you hold rate for 12 months?"

*What the agent does:* Helps you frame cost against value without making promises your contracts team hasn't approved. Uses the acknowledge → reframe → evidence → close structure for commercial objections.

**Stage 5 — Decision/Approval**
The deal is at final sign-off. Internal champions are seeking approval from leadership, procurement, or legal. The question has shifted from "which vendor" to "can we get this across the line."

*What they say:* "Our CFO wants to review before we sign." / "Legal is still reviewing the SOW." / "We're ready to start but need board approval." / "Can we start with a pilot while the full contract is finalized?"

*What the agent does:* Shifts to decision-enablement. Surfaces executive brief material, pilot framing, and risk-reduction language for internal champions to use when presenting to their leadership.

### How to Identify a Prospect's Stage

Give the agent this prompt at the start of any meeting prep:

> "Help me identify what buyer stage [Company Name] is in. Here's what I know: [2–3 sentences about what the prospect has said or done so far, how the lead came in, and what they've asked for]."

The agent will return a stage classification with the signals it used and the gaps it still needs. If the stage is unclear, it will tell you what one question would resolve the ambiguity.

### **The One Rule: Stage Mismatch Is the #1 Deal Killer.**

Sending a Commercial Validation buyer a Solution Exploration deck kills deals. Presenting proof-heavy slide decks to a Problem Identification buyer — who hasn't decided they need a staffing partner yet — sounds like a sales pitch when they need a consultant. Showing cost-efficiency content to a Vendor Evaluation buyer who is comparing you on delivery capability makes you look like the cheap option.

Every time you use the BD Agent, confirm the buyer's stage before selecting deck sections, discovery questions, or proof points.

## Part 2: Account Discovery

### What to Give the Agent

Before starting an Account Discovery session, have these five inputs ready:

1. **Company name** — full legal name preferred, plus any DBA or brand name
2. **Company size** — employee count or revenue band (approximate is fine)
3. **Industry / vertical** — be specific: "hospital system" not just "healthcare"
4. **Role of your contact** — their title and where they sit in the org (operational, HR, procurement, executive)
5. **Source of the lead** — inbound referral, cold outreach, conference, existing client referral, partner referral

**Example prompt:**

> "Run account discovery for Northgate Health System. They are a 3,200-employee regional hospital network in the Southwest. My contact is their VP of Human Resources. Lead came in through a referral from our existing client at Mesa Regional."

### What the Agent Produces

- **Stakeholder map** — who influences the staffing decision (HR, finance, operations, clinical leadership), which stakeholders you likely have access to, and which ones you don't
- **Environment summary** — what the organization likely looks like as a staffing buyer based on their size, type, and vertical
- **Likely pain points** — inferred from what companies like this typically experience, labeled clearly as inferences (not confirmed facts)
- **Account risk flags** — things that might complicate a deal (MSP/VMS requirement, incumbent vendor strength, geography constraints)
- **Next discovery priorities** — the most important things to confirm or rule out in your first conversation

### The 3-Step Verification Process

The account discovery output is a starting point, not a fact sheet.

**Step 1 — Check the company size claim.** The agent infers from the information you give it. If it says "likely MSP-managed based on their size," verify whether they actually use a VMS before walking in with a vendor-neutrality pitch.

**Step 2 — Verify your contact's role.** A VP of HR at a hospital is often an operational influencer, not the economic buyer. The agent will flag this. Confirm who owns the staffing budget before your first call.

**Step 3 — Cross-reference pain points against your own discovery notes.** If the agent infers "likely struggling with credentialing delays" and your referral contact mentioned something about compliance issues, you have signal. If nothing in your notes suggests that pain, hold it loosely.

## Part 3: Opportunity Qualification

### The Fit Model

The BD Agent evaluates every opportunity across ten dimensions:

| Dimension | What It Measures |
|---|---|
| Service Fit | How well the required work matches what Hire'in delivers |
| Domain Fit | Match to Healthcare, IT, Engineering, or General experience |
| Buyer Pain Severity | How urgent and material the problem is |
| Access Quality | Strength of your sponsor and access to the economic buyer |
| Delivery Feasibility | Geography, volume, skill scarcity, timeline, capacity |
| Commercial Attractiveness | Margin, placement potential, deal duration, expansion value |
| Strategic Value | Brand value, new geography, partner, or vertical |
| Proof Availability | Approved case studies and evidence that apply here |
| Compliance Readiness | Ability to meet program-specific requirements |
| Competitive Position | Differentiation and incumbent strength |

The agent returns a **Tier classification**:

- **Tier 1** — Strong fit across most dimensions. Pursue actively. This opportunity deserves your best preparation.
- **Tier 2** — Partial fit. Worth pursuing but with realistic expectations. Identify the weakest dimensions and address them first.
- **Long Shot** — Significant gaps in one or more critical dimensions. Do not invest heavily without a second opinion.

### How to Use the Qualification Output

**Spend 80% of your preparation time on Tier 1 opportunities.** The Tier 2 and Long Shot work may feel urgent, but the math doesn't support equal time investment.

**For a Long Shot:** Before investing further, copy the qualification output and share it with a senior BD lead. Ask specifically: "The agent scored this as a Long Shot on [dimension]. What am I missing, or should I redirect this to nurture?" This is not about giving up — it is about not spending six hours preparing for an opportunity where Hire'in has a fundamental gap.

**To prompt qualification:**

> "Qualify this opportunity. Company: [Name]. Domain: [Healthcare / IT / Engineering / General]. Pain: [1–2 sentences]. Contact: [Title]. Competing against: [incumbent or other vendors]. Timeline: [if known]."

## Part 4: Meeting Preparation

### The Three Questions

Before the agent generates your prep pack, it will ask you three questions. Your answers determine the entire output quality. Know these before you prompt:

1. **What is the meeting type?** (First discovery / Follow-up / Presentation / Proposal / Negotiation)
2. **What stage is this prospect?** (Use your stage classification from Part 1)
3. **What is the primary pain point you are addressing?** (Be specific — "time to fill" is more useful than "staffing problems")

### What the Agent Prepares

**Agenda** — A suggested meeting agenda with time allocations, sequenced to match the buyer's stage. A Problem Identification buyer gets an agenda built around discovery. A Vendor Evaluation buyer gets an agenda built around proof and differentiation.

**Discovery question set** — Questions organized by purpose: questions that confirm pain, questions that surface the economic buyer, questions that reveal the decision process, questions that identify competitive threats.

**Proof plan** — Which Insights articles, deck sections, or proof points to have ready, matched to what the buyer is likely to ask or object to.

### The One Rule for Discovery Questions

**Never use AI-generated discovery questions verbatim.** Read every question the agent produces. Own it. Adapt it to your voice and the specific conversation you expect to have. A question that sounds like it was read off a script breaks rapport. A question that sounds like it came from a practitioner who genuinely wants to understand the buyer's situation builds it.

The agent gives you the framework. You deliver it as yourself.

## Part 5: Deck Customization

### What a Master Deck Is

A Master Deck is the approved, locked version of a Hire'in capability presentation for a given domain (Healthcare, IT, Engineering, Professional Services, General). It contains the approved slide structure, approved capability claims, and approved positioning language for that domain.

**You cannot edit a Master Deck directly.** This is intentional. The Master Deck is the source of truth. Any change to a Master Deck requires super admin approval. This is how Hire'in protects itself from unsupported claims going to clients.

What you can do is request a **client-specific customization** — a new deck derived from the Master, with every slide rewritten for your specific client, buyer stage, and positioning angle.

### The Master-to-Client Workflow

**Step 1 — Identify the right Master Deck.**
Choose the domain that matches your prospect. If the deal spans domains (a hospital with an IT modernization project), choose the primary domain and note the secondary in the context field.

**Step 2 — Request customization.**
From the BD Agent chat, click "Build targeted client deck" on any account discovery or qualification response, or go directly to the Decks tab. You will be asked for:
- Client name
- Domain
- Positioning angle (choose from: Full Staffing Partner, Delivery Partner, RPO/Embedded Team, Domain Specialist, Cost Efficiency, or Custom)
- Context (paste in the buyer stage, primary pain, and key facts from your account discovery output — the agent pre-fills this for you from the conversation)

**Step 3 — AI customization.**
The agent rewrites every slide in the Master Deck to reflect the client's world. Generic bullets become specific to the client's industry, size, and pain. Speaker notes prep you for what to say on each slide with this specific buyer in mind.

**Step 4 — Review.**
Read the deck. Check every slide. The agent makes it specific, but you know things the agent doesn't. Edit any bullet that doesn't ring true. Add anything the conversation revealed. Remove anything that doesn't apply.

**Step 5 — Submit for approval.**
Click "Submit for approval." The deck enters a pending state. A super admin reviews it before it can go external.

**Step 6 — Export.**
Once approved, download as PDF or PPTX. Both formats are available from the deck viewer. PDF exports every slide as a rendered image at presentation quality. PPTX preserves the slide structure for further edits if needed.

### The Approval Gate Rule

**No deck leaves the building without super admin approval. Period.**

This is not a bureaucratic hurdle. It is how Hire'in protects the company from delivering promises to clients that operations cannot fulfill. If a customized deck contains a claim that is not supportable, the approval step catches it before the client sees it.

If your deck is urgent, contact your super admin directly. The approval queue shows every pending deck in order.

## Part 6: Using Insights Articles as Proof Points

### How It Works

The BD Agent automatically retrieves relevant published Insights articles based on the domain of your conversation. When you are in a Healthcare conversation, the agent pulls Healthcare articles. IT conversations pull IT articles. You do not need to prompt this — it happens in the background on every response.

### How to Use It Actively

When you want to find a specific article for an objection or capability claim, prompt the agent like this:

> "Find me an article that supports the claim that credential-aware screening reduces time to start for travel nurses."

or

> "Is there a published piece on how MSP programs affect staffing quality? I need something to reference in a meeting next week."

The agent will return matching articles with titles, excerpts, and guidance on how to use them in conversation.

### How to Reference an Article in a Meeting

**Use this format:** "According to our recent piece on healthcare staffing trends, published May 2026..."

**Do not:** Paste the article URL or say "I saw this on our website." Cite the article title and publish date like a practitioner who reads the material, not like someone running a browser tab.

**Read the article before you cite it.** The agent retrieves based on topic match. The article may support your point, or it may support a slightly different version of the point. Know what it says before you bring it up with a prospect.

### Why This Is More Credible Than a Generic Stat

A generic stat ("studies show fill rates improve by 30% with structured intake") has no source the buyer can verify. A published article from Hire'in's own Insights library, cited by title, tells the buyer two things: Hire'in thinks carefully about these problems, and Hire'in publishes its thinking. That is more credible than a percentage from an unnamed study.

## Part 7: Objection Handling

### The Five Most Common Objections

The BD Agent prepares responses for each of these in Meeting Preparation mode. The structure the agent follows: **Acknowledge → Reframe → Evidence → Close.**

**Objection 1: "Your rate is higher than our incumbent."**
- Acknowledge: "I understand rate matters, and I want to make sure we're comparing the right things."
- Reframe: Move the conversation from rate to total cost: time-to-fill, submission quality, the cost of a bad placement, the administrative burden of managing low-submission-quality vendors.
- Evidence: Reference a specific Hire'in quality control (credentialing discipline, submission review process) that prevents the downstream cost events.
- Close: "The question I'd want to answer together is: what is a bad submission costing you per incident, versus what you save per fill on rate?"

**Objection 2: "We already have an incumbent vendor."**
- Acknowledge: "That makes sense — you've built a relationship and a process."
- Reframe: Position as complementary or as a competitive alternative to test. "Most of our best clients kept their primary vendor for the roles they're reliable on and brought us in for the harder fills or specialty roles where they've been struggling."
- Evidence: The agent will surface relevant proof points about Hire'in's performance in situations where incumbents underperformed.
- Close: "Would it be worth testing us on two or three open positions that your current vendor hasn't been able to close? That gives you real data."

**Objection 3: "We're going to build this capability internally."**
- Acknowledge: "That's a serious investment. What's the timeline and scope you're thinking about?"
- Reframe: Build-versus-buy is a fair question. The real question is what interim coverage looks like and whether internal capability can scale to match demand peaks.
- Evidence: Reference the time and cost to build sourcing pipelines, credentialing workflows, and compliance infrastructure.
- Close: "While you're building, what does the coverage problem look like? Is that a 3-month gap or a 24-month gap?"

**Objection 4: "We have data security concerns about using an AI-powered vendor."**
- Acknowledge: "That's exactly the right question to ask. Let me tell you specifically how we handle candidate and client data."
- Reframe: Distinguish between AI in the Hire'in internal workflow (candidate sourcing, submission quality review) and data shared with the client. Client data does not go into a training model.
- Evidence: Ask your super admin for the current data handling documentation before this meeting.
- Close: "What specific data types are you concerned about? I want to make sure our security team gives you a direct answer, not a general one."

**Objection 5: "Your implementation timeline doesn't work for us."**
- Acknowledge: "Tell me what the hard deadline is."
- Reframe: Break the timeline into phases. What can start immediately, and what needs the full onboarding? Most clients can begin seeing submissions within days on high-urgency roles.
- Evidence: Reference Hire'in's intake and activation process.
- Close: "If we could have your first submissions in your hands by [date], would the onboarding timeline still be the issue, or is there something else driving the constraint?"

## Part 8: Executive Briefing Draft

### When to Use It

After any significant meeting: a first discovery call, a major presentation, a commercial negotiation. The executive brief is for your internal leadership, not the client.

Use the agent to draft it in under 5 minutes before the memory of the meeting fades.

### What to Give the Agent

> "Draft an executive brief for a meeting I just had with [Company Name]. Here's what happened: [3–5 bullet points covering: who attended, what they told us, what we proposed, what they responded, and what the next step is]. Buyer stage: [Stage]. Opportunity tier: [Tier 1/2/Long Shot]."

### What the Agent Produces

- Account and opportunity summary (2–3 sentences)
- Key findings from the meeting
- Risk factors
- Decisions needed from leadership
- Recommended next step and owner
- Suggested timeline

### Before You Send It

Read it. Fix the names. The agent does not know which VP was in the room unless you told it. Fix any detail that does not match your recollection of the meeting. This goes to leadership — accuracy matters more than speed.

## Part 9: Follow-Up Communication

### How to Prompt It

> "Draft a follow-up email for my meeting with [Name] at [Company]. Key things we discussed: [2–3 specific points]. Next step I promised: [specific action]. Tone: [professional / warm / direct]."

The more specific you are about what was actually discussed, the more personalized the output. Generic inputs produce generic emails.

### What the Agent Produces

A follow-up email that references specific things from the meeting (if you provided them), confirms the next step, and closes with a clear action.

### The One Mistake

**Never send an AI-generated follow-up without reading every word, including the prospect's name and company name.**

The agent uses the information you give it. If you mistype a name, the email will go out with the error. If the prospect's company goes by a different name than what you entered, that will be in the email. Read it. Own it. Then send it.

## Part 10: Pre-Meeting Preparation Checklist

Run these 8 steps before every client meeting using the BD Agent.

**Step 1 — Start a conversation in the correct domain.**
Open the BD Agent and start a new conversation. Select the domain that matches your prospect: Healthcare, IT, Engineering, Professional Services, or General. This determines which Insights articles and capability knowledge the agent loads.

**Step 2 — Run Account Discovery.**
Paste in: company name, size, industry/vertical, contact role, lead source. Get the stakeholder map and environment summary. Verify the output against what you already know.

**Step 3 — Classify the buyer stage.**
Ask the agent to classify the buyer's stage based on what you know so far. If you don't have enough signal, identify the one question you will ask at the start of the meeting to resolve the ambiguity.

**Step 4 — Run Opportunity Qualification.**
Get the Tier classification. If Long Shot, get a second opinion from a senior BD lead before continuing. If Tier 1 or 2, proceed.

**Step 5 — Run Meeting Preparation.**
Tell the agent the meeting type, buyer stage, and primary pain point. Get the agenda, discovery questions, and proof plan.

**Step 6 — Review the discovery questions.**
Read every question. Rewrite any that don't sound like you. Cut any that are redundant or premature for this stage of the relationship.

**Step 7 — Pull the proof points.**
Ask the agent to find Insights articles relevant to the expected conversation. Read the articles before the meeting. Know what they say, not just what the agent says they say.

**Step 8 — Check your deck.**
If you are presenting, confirm the deck is approved. If you need a customized version, submit the customization request at least 24 hours before the meeting to allow time for approval.

## Part 11: Deal Stage Playbook

For each of the five buyer stages: what to prepare, what to ask, which deck sections to emphasize, and what your next step commitment should be.

---

### Stage 1 — Problem Identification

**What to prepare:**
- Account discovery output and stakeholder map
- 3–4 questions that help the buyer quantify the cost of their current problem
- One Insights article that validates the pain they've described — do not lead with it, have it ready if the conversation opens the door

**What to ask:**
- "What does a bad week look like for your staffing team right now?"
- "When a position goes unfilled for 30 days, what does that cost you operationally?"
- "Who feels the impact of this problem most acutely in your organization?"
- "Have you looked at how you'd solve this, or are you still figuring out what the problem actually is?"

**Which deck sections to emphasize:**
None — this is a listening meeting, not a presentation. If you must share something, share an Insights article that validates their problem. Bringing a full capability deck to a Problem Identification meeting signals that you aren't listening.

**Next step commitment:**
A follow-up conversation where you present a specific operating model that could address their situation. Do not propose a solution, pricing, or vendor comparison at this stage.

---

### Stage 2 — Solution Exploration

**What to prepare:**
- Operating model overview for your domain
- Comparison of staffing approaches (MSP, direct vendor, embedded team, RPO)
- 2–3 Insights articles on the model that best fits their situation
- Qualification output so you know which dimensions of fit to address proactively

**What to ask:**
- "What delivery model are you leaning toward, or are you still figuring that out?"
- "What has and hasn't worked with your current approach to this?"
- "Who else is part of the conversation when you think about changing your staffing model?"
- "What would make you confident you're choosing the right model?"

**Which deck sections to emphasize:**
How Hire'in operates, the AI-and-human delivery model, service model options (contract, direct hire, MSP/VMS support, embedded team). Avoid heavy proof slides — the buyer is not yet comparing vendors, so credentialing metrics and fill-rate data are premature.

**Next step commitment:**
A session where you walk them through what a Hire'in engagement would look like specifically for their environment. Bring one relevant proof story — not a full deck.

---

### Stage 3 — Vendor Evaluation

**What to prepare:**
- Full presentation deck (approved client-specific version — submit customization request at least 24 hours before)
- 2–3 proof points or reference stories relevant to their vertical and pain
- Objection preparation for the five most common buyer concerns at this stage (rate, incumbent vendor, implementation timeline, data security, internal build)
- Qualification output to know which dimensions are weakest and address them before the buyer raises them

**What to ask:**
- "What does a successful vendor look like to you six months in?"
- "What concerns do you have that we haven't addressed yet?"
- "What has disqualified vendors in conversations like this before?"
- "Who besides you is involved in making this decision, and what matters most to them?"

**Which deck sections to emphasize:**
Proof, differentiation, risk reduction, quality controls, implementation readiness. This is the moment for evidence, not education. Emphasize what makes Hire'in different from the alternatives — not just what Hire'in does.

**Next step commitment:**
A reference call, a shortlist decision, or a meeting with the economic buyer — with a specific date attached. If they won't commit to a date, find out what's blocking it.

---

### Stage 4 — Commercial Validation

**What to prepare:**
- Clear understanding of your rate structure and what flexibility exists (check with leadership before the meeting)
- Objection handling for rate, SLA, and guarantee conversations
- Reframe of cost as total cost of staffing (fill quality, administrative burden, incumbent underperformance) versus unit rate
- Executive brief from your previous meeting to refresh both sides on what was agreed

**What to ask:**
- "What rate are you benchmarking us against, and where does that number come from?"
- "What SLAs are in your current contract with your incumbent?"
- "If we can't move on rate, are there other terms that would help you get this across the line internally?"
- "Is there a pilot scope we could agree on at lower risk while you validate quality before the full commitment?"

**Which deck sections to emphasize:**
ROI framing, cost-per-quality-fill versus cost-per-submission, pilot scoping. Keep slides minimal here — this is a negotiation, not a presentation. Two or three comparison slides at most.

**Next step commitment:**
A draft agreement or SOW for review by both legal teams, with a named owner on each side and a review deadline.

---

### Stage 5 — Decision/Approval

**What to prepare:**
- A clean executive brief summarizing the opportunity for internal use by your champion
- Pilot framing if the full contract is stalled — a small initial scope that lets the client start while approvals complete
- Risk-reduction language: what commitments are bounded, what is reviewable, what can be paused
- Your own internal leadership alignment — make sure your team is ready to start the moment the contract is signed

**What to ask:**
- "What does your champion need to present internally to get this approved?"
- "Is it the budget, the scope, the legal terms, or something else holding things up?"
- "Would a pilot scope at lower commitment help you get started while the full agreement is finalized?"
- "What would make this a yes in the next two weeks?"

**Which deck sections to emphasize:**
One-page summary for the decision-maker (not the full deck), pilot or phased implementation plan, escalation and exit clause clarity. Your champion is selling internally on your behalf — give them one clean, brief document they can put in front of their CFO or board.

**Next step commitment:**
A signed agreement or a signed pilot SOW with a start date for the first role — or a named date by which the decision will be made.

## Part 12: Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Skipping Account Discovery

**What BD teams do:** Jump straight to meeting prep or deck customization without running Account Discovery first.

**The consequence:** Your prep is built on assumptions about who the buyer is and what they care about. You walk in with the wrong framing, ask questions the buyer has already answered publicly, and miss the stakeholder you actually needed to know about.

**The fix:** Run Account Discovery for every new prospect, no matter how good the referral seems. It takes 3 minutes.

---

### Mistake 2: Using the Wrong Buyer Stage

**What BD teams do:** Pick a stage that matches where they want the deal to be, not where the buyer actually is.

**The consequence:** You bring Vendor Evaluation content to a Solution Exploration buyer. They feel sold to before they've made a decision. The deal slows down or goes cold.

**The fix:** Let the agent classify the stage, not you. If you disagree with the classification, tell the agent why. It will revise. The conversation about stage is valuable — it forces you to articulate what signals you have.

---

### Mistake 3: Sending Unreviewed AI Content Externally

**What BD teams do:** Generate a follow-up email or executive brief, glance at it, and send it without reading it carefully.

**The consequence:** A misspelled prospect name, a wrong company name, a claim that doesn't apply to this buyer, or a commitment you didn't actually make — all in writing, to the prospect.

**The fix:** Read every word before you send anything. The agent is fast. You provide the judgment.

---

### Mistake 4: Not Using Insights Articles as Proof

**What BD teams do:** Tell prospects about Hire'in's capabilities without providing any published evidence to back the claims.

**The consequence:** Claims without proof sound like marketing. Prospects at Vendor Evaluation and beyond discount unsupported claims from every vendor they talk to. Published thought leadership changes that dynamic.

**The fix:** For every capability claim you make in a meeting, ask the agent whether there's a published article that supports it. Use it.

---

### Mistake 5: Not Logging the Meeting Outcome

**What BD teams do:** Finish the meeting, skip the executive brief, and make a mental note to follow up.

**The consequence:** No institutional memory of what was said. If the deal stalls for 60 days, you start the next conversation from scratch. If you hand the account to someone else, they have no context.

**The fix:** Spend 5 minutes with the agent immediately after the meeting. Draft the executive brief. Save it. The next meeting is always better when you remember what happened in the last one.
